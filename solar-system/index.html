    Guide

    Everything you see is computed for the displayed instant: where each body is, which way it faces, where its shadow falls and which stars are behind it. Nothing is animated along a canned path.

    Getting around

    • Drag to rotate, scroll or pinch to zoom, right-drag, shift-drag or arrow keys to pan.
    • Click a body, its label, or its name in the list to fly to it. Follow keeps the camera travelling with it.
    • Space play/pause · , . one day back/forward · [ ] slower/faster · R reverse · N now · T scale · Esc back to the overview.

    Scale

    At true scale the Solar System is almost entirely empty: if the Sun were a grapefruit, Earth would be a grain of sand 13 m away and Neptune 390 m away. The overview shrinks distances and enlarges bodies so everything fits on one screen, and the slider morphs smoothly between the two. Directions are never distorted, so alignments such as eclipses and conjunctions look right at every setting. Rings around tiny dots mark bodies too small to see.

    Shadows and eclipses

    Shadows are computed for every point on a surface from the true sizes and distances of the Sun and of the body in the way, including the Sun’s limb darkening and the light’s travel time (the sunlight hitting Earth passed the Moon 1.3 s earlier, which moves a solar eclipse 35 km along its track). They are therefore correct at every scale setting, even when the Moon is drawn closer than it really is. During a lunar eclipse the Moon turns copper-red, because the only light reaching it has been bent and reddened by Earth’s atmosphere. Saturn’s rings cast shadows on the planet and Saturn casts one on its rings. Jupiter’s moons cast theirs on Jupiter and disappear into its shadow.

    Boost shadow exaggerates faint partial shadows, which are real but hard to see. It is off by default.

    How accurate is it?

    The positions were compared with NASA JPL’s DE440 ephemeris (via Horizons) over 1800–2050:

    WhatLargest error, 1800–2050
    Planets as seen from Earth< 25″ (the eye resolves ~60″)
    Moon< 21 km (12″)
    Io, Europa, Ganymede, Callisto< 920 km (≤ 0.12° of their orbits)
    Titan · Phobos · Deimos< 900 km · 20 km · 56 km
    Solar eclipses (1919, 1999, 2024, 2027)greatest eclipse within 5 km and 5 s of NASA’s canon

    Outside 1800–2050 the planets stay good to about an arcminute for centuries. The moons of Mars and Saturn are extrapolated fits, and Earth’s rotation depends on ΔT, which becomes uncertain far from the present (it is only predictable a few years ahead, so future eclipse tracks can shift east–west by tens of km). The date badge says which regime you are in.

    Some things are representative rather than exact: cloud patterns on Earth, Jupiter and the other giants are snapshots, and brightness is adjusted so distant worlds stay visible (Neptune receives 1/900 of the sunlight Earth does). Phobos and Deimos are drawn as ellipsoids of their true dimensions.
